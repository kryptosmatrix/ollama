The ruling on three findings — a warning channel

Ash asked for one ruling covering three findings left open: a credential in
stdio Args, a credential in a URL query string, and docker runtime arguments
supplied by a registry publisher.

FIRST, A CORRECTION TO MY OWN FRAMING

I had said three times that these share one shape. Two of them do: a credential
the user has put somewhere Ollama cannot protect. The docker case is different
in kind — a third party's flags widening what gets run. That is code-execution
scope, not credential exposure, and I should have separated them earlier rather
than repeating a tidy sentence.

SECOND, AND THE ACTUAL RULING: THE CHOICE WAS FALSE

I had framed each as refuse-or-allow. Refusing strands a user who has no safe
alternative — the pattern that catches "--api-key=sk-live-..." also catches
"--token-file=/etc/creds", and expanding an environment reference into argv puts
the secret in the process list, which is the thing being avoided. Allowing in
silence lets a user believe nothing was given away.

The reason I could not choose was that Problems() answers yes or no and there
was nowhere to say anything else. That is a missing mechanism, not a genuine
dilemma, and I let it decide a security question for four exchanges. KANON 5
says extend the honest path rather than work around it; the honest path here was
one that did not exist yet.

Config.Warnings() now answers a different question from Problems(). A problem is
"this cannot work". A warning is "this works, and here is what it costs you".

WHAT IS WARNED ABOUT, AND WHY EACH IS A WARNING RATHER THAN A REFUSAL

  Args carrying a value under a credential-shaped name — "any program running
  as you can read it from the process list". Not refused, because the pattern
  cannot tell a secret from a path, and the user with a real secret has no safe
  alternative to be sent to.

  A credential-shaped query parameter in a server URL — "stored in mcp.json as
  written and travels with the file". Not refused, for the same reason: no
  environment-reference mechanism exists for URLs, unlike headers. Userinfo in
  a URL stays a hard refusal, because that is unambiguous and a header with
  ${env:NAME} is a real alternative.

  Container flags that reach outside the container — -v, --mount, --privileged,
  --net=host and their kin, each named with what it does. Not refused, because
  a server that legitimately needs a volume is ordinary, and the user is the one
  who has to weigh it.

An ordinary server produces no warning at all. A channel that fires on
everything is a channel nobody reads.

WHERE IT IS SHOWN — WHICH IS THE PART THAT MATTERS

At approval, in both surfaces, next to the thing being agreed to. Also in
`ollama mcp list` and on the MCP Servers page. A warning that exists and is not
shown where the decision is made would be a mechanism rather than a protection,
which is the failure this whole review has been finding in other guises.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT CLAIM

It does not make any of the three safe. A credential on a command line is still
readable from the process list. What changes is that the person who put it there
is told, at the moment they are deciding, in words that say what happens rather
than what rule was matched.

"failed" — on a compile error, because the local variable became unused. That is
the compiler talking, not the test, and it proves nothing about behaviour. The
re-run keeps the variable alive and is the honest one.
